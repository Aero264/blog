In search of my Wentworth

Austen’s heroines seem to get all the attention. So let’s break from tradition. Who’s your favourite among Austen’s heroes? Mr Darcy? A bit cliche, don’t you think? My favourite is Frederick Wentworth.

Frederick Wentworth was 31 years old when he returned to Somerset. He has had an illustrious career in the Royal Navy and has returned to the same place where he was rejected by the love of his life.

I returned to Britain, in the aftermath of my mother’s passing on the eve of my 31st birthday. Together with academic bullying and abrupt resignation, it made a lot of sense to have not returned at all. But Britain is where I first shook hands with the reality of life. Fresh out of grad school, my first job was in Britain. I arrived weeks before the onset of a global pandemic. First stop, Bristol. First job, helicopter dynamics. And what followed has been an eventful journey, and my life’s better because of it.

Over the next seven years, I have come to meet a lot of Anne Elliots, quite a few Lady Russells, some brush ins with William Elliots, the acquaintance of Walter Elliots. But I am yet to meet a Frederick Wentworth.

I first read Austen in school. Pride and prejudice. Though times and technology have changed, my experience of Britain is very much in line of how I imagined Austen’s world to be. But I never expected it to come into conflict with mine. I’m the first graduate in my extended family, eventually going on to get a PhD from India’s best engineering institution. My twin sister, despite having failed a year in school, eventually went on to do a Postgraduate degree in Economics. My cousin, who was forced to leave school due to financial problems, got a job at 19, worked hard, and now has an MBA. Tamilnadu is in no way perfect, but it instilled a strong belief that everything is possible.

That’s why I was surprised to learn about a culture where even the best and brightest minds in the country had to choose between applying to Oxford and Cambridge, and not have an opportunity to pursue both. A culture that has come to clip its own wings because it’s afraid of what lies beyond the horizon. Fresh out of school, the first decision a child makes is a constraint dressed up as choice. And therefore, it is quite natural that I have had the pleasure of meeting people of every hue and shade but for the ones of Frederick Wentworth.

In Austen’s world, he’s the outsider, a man of no rank. Anne is Britain’s beloved. She has rank, means well, knows better, and defers to authority. Her refusal at nineteen can be forgiven: social wisdom prevailed over youthful reservation. But after eight years of constancy, when he arrived in front of her, she could only pronounce a moral judgement on the constancy of men, but could not utter a single word to the one who mattered. Such pusillanimity!

Just as there are no villains in Anne’s story, Britain too is persuaded by the unwritten rules of society, and held back by well-meaning people who are reluctant to act. Anne’s eventual happiness came not because she waited, but because Wentworth risked his heart again: “You pierce my soul. I am half agony, half hope.” The finest declaration of love in English came because he was willing to stake everything a second time.

But the idea of Wentworth seems to have become alien to Britain. What I see instead is a cultivated state of helplessness. Even Jarvis’s portrayal of Wentworth in the latest adaptation of Persuasion could not escape it.

I made an unwavering bet on Britain a second time. Academic bullying denied me the opportunity to pursue my own grant. I fought this as a self-represented Claimant in the Employment Tribunal, which culminated in the Tribunal making a sharp oral remark in a public preliminary hearing that “A fraud is a fraud and they do not need any more evidence to see that”. This prompted a settlement before the final hearing. Within months of moving to a new job, I secured a grant as Principal Investigator and led my first clinical study.

But fulfilment is not just accolades and achievements. Wentworth himself realised this and went to Bath. And, in ten days, I am leaving Britain for India, in search of my Wentworth.

Au revoir, Britain. Should I ever find my Captain Wentworth here, I shall return again.
